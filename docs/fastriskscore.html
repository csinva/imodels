FastRiskScore: Fitting sparse integer risk scores via Autoresearch

Chandan Singh · September 2026

📄 Paper, 🗂 Doc, 📌 Citation

A risk score predicts by adding up a few small integer points, one for each feature that applies, and reading the risk for the total off a table. Clinical scores such as CHA2DS2-VASc are built this way because a person can compute and check them by hand. Finding the best score for a dataset is a hard integer problem: RiskSLIM solves it exactly with a commercial solver but slowly, and FasterRisk solves it approximately in seconds. Using autoresearch, we build FastRiskScore, which on held-out datasets fits scores about 50× faster than FasterRisk with a slightly lower training loss and the same test AUC. On small problems where every possible score can be enumerated, it finds the best one in 49 of 50 cases, against 28 of 50 for FasterRisk.

1. Quickstart & example usage

from imodels import FastRiskScoreClassifier
from sklearn.datasets import load_breast_cancer
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split

X, y = load_breast_cancer(return_X_y=True, as_frame=True)
X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(X, y, random_state=42)

model = FastRiskScoreClassifier(k=5).fit(X_train, y_train)
proba = model.predict_proba(X_test)

k is the most features the score may use and max_points (default 5) bounds the points of each. Numeric columns are split at their deciles and categorical ones one-hot encoded, so the score's lines read as conditions on the original columns. Printing the model gives the whole score:

Class 1 is a benign tumour. This score reaches a test AUC of 0.984 and was fit in 0.03 s. The fitted model also exposes the points and the risk map directly:

model.points_           # {"worst area <= 906.6": 5, ...}, the lines of the score
model.total_score(X)    # the total points of each row
model.risk(score)       # 1 / (1 + exp(-(model.scale_ * score + model.intercept_)))
model.train_loss_       # the mean training log loss of that risk
How the risk for a total is chosen

The points fix only the ranking of rows. The risk for each total comes from a logistic curve, risk(s) = 1 / (1 + exp(-(a·s + b))), whose two numbers a and b are fit to the training data after the points are chosen. The points are chosen to make that fitted curve's log loss as small as possible (Sec 3), so the table in the printout is calibrated on the training data by construction. RiskSLIM and FasterRisk print the same kind of table; they write b as an integer offset and 1/a as a multiplier.

2. Experimental results

FastRiskScore was produced by the autoresearch loop of Agentic-imodels (code on github). Two coding agents were each given the same starting solver, FasterRisk rewritten as one Python file, and the same fixed benchmark, and ran independently for about 90 minutes. One was asked to lower the loss, the other to go faster. Each proposed a change, ran the benchmark, and kept the change only if it lowered the loss without slowing down by more than 10%, or sped up by at least 10% without raising the loss. The benchmark (the visible set) has 70 problems: 14 datasets × 5 values of k (3, 4, 5, 7 and 10), each with a 60 s limit, run one per core.

Every solver is scored the same way. It returns integer points in [−5, 5] on at most k of a dataset's binary features; the benchmark then fits the risk curve for those points itself and records the training log loss of that curve (the criterion), the test AUC of the total score on a held-out 20% split, and the fit time. Because the benchmark computes the loss from the points alone, a solver cannot score well by misreporting.

Extra details on experimental settings

Scoring. The benchmark rejects any answer whose points are not integers, fall outside [−5, 5] or use more than k features; no solver in the figure had such an answer. Given the points, the risk curve is fit by Newton's method on the two numbers a and b, a convex problem. Each solver process first fits a tiny warm-up problem, untimed, so numba compilation is not counted; FastRiskScore's first fit on a new machine takes about 30 s longer for that reason.

Best known losses. The excess loss is measured against the lowest loss any integer solver reached on the problem in any of these runs, including FasterRisk with every search width raised (a beam of 50 × 50, a pool of 200 and 100 multipliers) and a 20-minute limit, which takes about 12× its default time.

RiskSLIM. The free CPLEX Community Edition refuses problems above 1,000 variables or constraints, and RiskSLIM's cutting planes pass that limit on wider datasets; it returned no score on 25 of 70 visible problems and 102 of 135 hidden ones, and is charged the base rate there. With a full CPLEX licence it would do better, but it certifies optimality only on small problems within these limits either way, as the FasterRisk paper also found. RiskSLIM and SLIM run until their time limit on most problems, so their times sit near 60 s.

Baselines. The search tested each candidate package in a clean Python 3.12 environment. abess and fastsparsegams install from PyPI; scikit-psl and okridge pin numpy < 2 and are vendored with small fixes (a numpy 2 incompatibility in scikit-psl, a call to a removed method in okridge), and scikit-psl gains a stop after k stages. AutoScore is only available in R, so its recipe (rank by random-forest importance, fit a logistic regression, divide by the smallest coefficient and round) is reimplemented on the binary features. RiskSLIM has no open-solver version, so its cutting-plane algorithm is reimplemented with HiGHS, re-solving the integer program each round and starting from rounded heuristic solutions as RiskSLIM does; it proves optimality on 6 of the 70 visible problems. Left out: scorepyo (abandoned, pinned to 2022 packages), weight-of-evidence scorecards (optbinning, scorecardpy; not sparse integer points), l0learn (no Python 3.12 build; fastsparsegams is the same code) and GroupFasterRisk (the same as FasterRisk without feature groups). Probabilistic scoring lists are killed at 3× the time limit on 10 visible and 51 hidden problems and charged the base rate there.

imodels' previous SLIMClassifier solves its integer program only with a commercial solver installed; without one, which is the default, it rounds an L2 logistic regression. Its penalty is searched here so that at most k points are nonzero, and points are clipped to [−5, 5].

C. One run per solver at k = 5 on each of the 27 datasets at full size, 27 at a time, with a 10-minute limit (killed at 30 minutes). RiskSLIM stops at the CPLEX Community Edition's size limit on 22 datasets; SLIM passes its limit on 25, since HiGHS finishes its current step before stopping, and on the remaining 2 returns a proven optimum of its 0-1 loss.

Both runs cut the time by more than 20× and lowered the loss. On the visible set (Fig 1A) the kept versions move steadily down and to the left. Run 2's final version fits a problem in 0.038 s against 1.49 s for FasterRisk and reaches a lower training loss on 54 of 70 problems (higher on 1). Its loss is also below that of FasterRisk with every search width raised, which takes 18 s a problem. The two runs, started from the same code with different goals, arrived at the same two main changes (Sec 3).

The speed-up holds on the hidden set; the loss gain is smaller there. On the 27 TabArena datasets (Fig 1B), FastRiskScore is 54× faster than FasterRisk (median over problems; slowest fit 0.51 s against 35 s) and reaches a lower loss on 90 of 135 problems, the same loss on 40 and a higher one on 5. The mean loss drops by only 0.00025, about a third of the gap between FasterRisk and a real-valued model with the same features. Much of the visible-set gain came from datasets with real-valued columns (spambase above all), where FasterRisk's rounding loses a feature; every hidden dataset is binarized, and there FasterRisk was already close to the best score.

No baseline comes close. The best of the rest are sparse real-valued models rounded by FasterRisk's rounding step: OKRidge's optimal support reaches a hidden-set loss of 0.3286 and abess and fastSparse 0.3303, against 0.3272 for FasterRisk and 0.3270 for FastRiskScore (Table 1); OKRidge also takes about 450× as long as FastRiskScore. The exact methods fall behind for a structural reason as much as a computational one. RiskSLIM's objective fixes the scale of the score, so on the 6 problems where the cutting planes prove RiskSLIM's optimum, that optimal score still has a higher loss than FastRiskScore's once the risk curve is refit (for example 0.0875 against 0.0659 on breastcancer at k = 10). This is the reason FasterRisk introduced its multiplier.

The ordering holds at full size. With every row kept (up to 120,000 training rows) and k = 5 (Fig 1C, Table 1), FastRiskScore takes 0.08 s a problem against 4.8 s for FasterRisk (50× faster by the median; slowest fit 1.0 s against 103 s), with a lower loss on 21 of the 27 datasets and a higher one on none. RiskSLIM returns no score on 22 of the 27 within the free CPLEX edition's limits, and SLIM runs out its 10 minutes on 25 and returns worse scores than rounding a logistic regression. Across all three panels the test AUC follows the training loss: FastRiskScore's is the highest of the integer scores, within 0.002 of the real-valued model it approximates.

On small problems it finds the best score. A benchmark can only compare solvers with each other, so we also generated 50 random problems small enough to enumerate every possible score (300 rows, 7 binary features, k = 3, points in [−3, 3]: 8,358 candidate scores each). FastRiskScore's score is the best one on 49 of the 50 (the other is 0.0008 above it), and FasterRisk's on 28 of the 50, with a mean gap to the best score 50 times larger (evolve_slim/benchmarks/exhaustive_check.py).

3. Methods: FastRiskScore

The problem is the one RiskSLIM and FasterRisk solve. Over integer points w with at most k nonzero entries, each in [−5, 5], minimise

\[ \min_{w}\;\; \min_{a,\,b\,\in\,\mathbb{R}}\; \underbrace{\frac{1}{n}\sum_{i=1}^{n} \log\!\left(1 + e^{-\tilde y_i\,(a\, x_i^\top w \,+\, b)}\right)}_{\text{log loss of the risk curve for the total score } x_i^\top w} \]

with \(\tilde y_i \in \{-1, +1\}\). The inner minimum over a and b is the risk curve of Sec 1. FasterRisk searches for good real-valued coefficients and then rounds them, choosing among a few multipliers 1/a with the intercept fixed by the rounding. FastRiskScore keeps FasterRisk's first stage, rebuilt for speed, and replaces its second stage with a search over the integer points themselves, each candidate scored by the loss above with a and b refit. Four stages:

  1. Compress the data. Identical rows (same features and label) are merged into one row with a count. Binary data has many: a dataset of 10,000 rows often has a few hundred distinct ones on the features a score uses.
  2. Beam search for real-valued solutions (FasterRisk's): grow the set of features one at a time, keeping the 10 best sets and extending each by the 10 features with the largest gradient. Each candidate is fit by Newton's method on its own features, where FasterRisk used coordinate descent over all rows. Each set keeps its rows grouped by their values on the chosen features, so a fit runs on a few hundred groups rather than every row.
  3. Round each of the 10 final solutions at 20 scales (the largest point from 0.5 to 5) and keep, for each, the rounding with the lowest loss after refitting a and b.
  4. Local search on the integer points from the 5 best roundings: change one feature's points to any value in [−5, 5], add a feature, or, when neither helps, swap a feature for another. Because the total score takes only a few distinct values, a move's loss is computed on a table of (score, feature value) cells rather than on rows. A cheap estimate ranks all moves, and the two best are checked exactly.

What the loop found

Searching the integer points directly, scored by the refit loss (both runs)

Both runs found this first and it made the largest difference to the loss: on the visible set it moved the mean excess loss from about +0.002 to −0.001 (relative to the original best known losses) in each run. FasterRisk's rounding is judged by the loss at a fixed multiplier, but a risk score is used with its best-fitting curve, so a rounding that looks worse at the fixed multiplier can be better once a and b are refit. On spambase at k = 3 FasterRisk's rounding sends one of its three features to zero, because the feature's scale makes its best integer point round to 0; the local search recovers it and lowers the loss by 0.095.

At first the local search added 60 to 80% to the run time, since every candidate move meant a new fit of a and b over all rows. Two changes made it cheap: computing the loss from counts of positive and negative rows per value of the total score (with 5 features the total takes at most 51 values), and ranking moves by an estimate before checking only the best two exactly. Run 2 found that its first estimate was too optimistic for moves that change the spread of the scores (0.58 estimated against 0.63 true on compas at k = 3), and fixed it by recomputing the cells a move touches after one Newton step in (a, b).

Where the time went, and how it was removed (mostly run 2)

Profiling FasterRisk on bank at k = 10 (19.9 s) showed 82% of the time in coordinate descent, which passes over every row for each coordinate step. Merging identical rows and fitting by Newton's method on the few chosen features cut the time 5× in both runs. Run 2 then grouped each beam candidate's rows by their values on its features (another 2.5×), moved the creation of each child candidate into one compiled call along with smaller changes (−34%), and batched the gradient and swap screens into single matrix products (−14%, then −12%), so that each step reads the data once rather than once per candidate.

The integer search also let the loop remove work from FasterRisk's first stage. Run 2 dropped FasterRisk's diverse pool (swapping each feature for up to 50 others) and started the local search from the 10 beam solutions instead, with a lower loss. Run 1 kept the pool but cut it from 50 to 10 solutions and the rounding multipliers from 20 to 5, each at no cost in loss.

What did not work
  • Local search from an empty score, without the beam: much worse (excess loss +0.010 against −0.001); the beam's choice of features matters.
  • Cheaper ways to rank beam candidates before fitting them (a one-step score test, a one-coordinate fit): all raised the loss by 0.0002 to 0.0007.
  • Smaller beams: 5 parents instead of 10 raised the loss by 0.003.
  • Random restarts and perturbations of the local search: large gains on a few stuck problems (mushroom at k = 7) but less than 0.0001 on average, for 2 to 3 times the time. 300 random restarts per problem on 15 problems beat the final version on only 4.
  • More starts, deeper swaps, rescaling all points at once, refitting the best set of features and rounding again at several resolutions: gains of at most 0.0002, for 20 to 60% more time, so none passed the keep rule.

FastRiskScore is a heuristic: unlike RiskSLIM with a full CPLEX licence, it does not prove that its score is the best one. It is deterministic, single-threaded, and needs numba, which compiles the search once per machine in about 30 seconds and caches it (set RISKSCORE_NUMBA_CACHE=0 if the package directory is not writable). A time limit (time_limit, 60 s by default) stops the search early on very large data and returns the best score found; on every dataset here it finished well within it.
