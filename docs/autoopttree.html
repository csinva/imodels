AutoOptTree: certifiably optimal decision trees via Autoresearch

Chandan Singh · September 2026

📄 Paper, 🗂 Doc, 📌 Citation

Standard decision trees are fit greedily, leading to tres that are larger than they need to be and harder to interpret. This has spawned a line of work seeking to find the smallest ("optimal") trees that best fit a dataset, e.g. GOSDT or STreeD. Here, we build on this work using autoresearch to develop a fast library for fitting small trees. We build AutoOptTree, a fast solver that achieves useful speedups on real-world problems for fitting small trees. It certifies optimality on 63 of 70 benchmark problems against 47 for GOSDT, and is 81× faster on geometric mean. The speedup comes from stronger admissible bounds and from compiling the search, so every tree it returns is still a proven optimum. On a held-out benchmark built from TabArena, which none of the development was done against, the ordering holds: 59 of 70 certified against GOSDT's 43, and 19× faster.

1. Quickstart & example usage

from imodels import AutoOptTreeClassifier
from sklearn.datasets import load_breast_cancer
from sklearn.model_selection import train_test_split

X, y = load_breast_cancer(return_X_y=True, as_frame=True)
X_train, X_test, y_train, y_test = train_test_split(X, y, random_state=42)

model = AutoOptTreeClassifier(regularization=0.05).fit(X_train, y_train)
preds = model.predict(X_test)

regularization is the only hyperparameter: larger regularization returns smaller trees.

The fitted model reports whether the search finished, and what it proved:

model.optimal_      # True: no other tree on these features scores better
model.objective_    # 0.1775, the proven minimum of error + 0.05 * leaves
model.n_leaves_     # 2
print(model)        # the tree, as nested if/else on the caller's feature names

At this price a leaf is expensive, and the best tree that exists on breast cancer is a single threshold, which reaches 89.5% on the held-out quarter:

if mean concave points >= 0.05128 then:
    class = 0
else:
    class = 1

optimal_ is the part greedy trees cannot offer. When it is True, the objective reported is a minimum over every tree that exists on the binarized features, not the best of those that happened to be tried. When the time limit is hit first it is False, the model warns, and lowerbound_ and upperbound_ bracket how much could still be left on the table.

What makes a problem expensive is the number of distinct values in the data, not the number of rows: each one is a candidate split. Breast cancer's 30 continuous columns become 11,513 binary features, and the search above certifies in 0.05 seconds only because a leaf priced at 0.05 rules most trees out immediately. Drop regularization to 0.02 and the same problem does not finish in a minute. Discretizing the features first, or pricing leaves higher, is what keeps an exact solver exact in practice.

Each development dataset has a stand-in built from a TabArena classification dataset by a fixed rule, not a choice per dataset: originals largest first, each taking the smallest unused source with enough rows and enough of each class. The stand-in matches the original's rows and features exactly and its class balance to within 0.05, and keeps the features with the most mutual information with the label. Binary originals get binarized features; numeric ones get quantile bins, one bin count per dataset chosen to match the original's number of binary splits. Gray figures are the original's targets. Two stand-ins fall short on splits because a small sample of their source has too few distinct values (chudi, iris), and the two one-feature stand-ins reach 88 to 92% of theirs because the originals are synthetic and have no tied values. TabArena's HELOC and in-vehicle coupon datasets are excluded, since the development suite already contains them. The builder, the manifest and the per-problem results are in agentic-imodels under baselines/benchmarks/external/.

AutoOptTree was produced by the autoresearch loop described in Agentic-imodels (code on github). An agent was given a working exact solver, a fixed benchmark, and a gate that no change may ever return a tree that is not optimal; it proposed bounds, argued them admissible, measured them, and kept the ones that won. The Methods section re-proves every bound from first principles and finds two of the loop's arguments that do not hold; neither reaches the shipped model.

The benchmark is 70 problems: 14 datasets crossed with five values of regularization (0.1, 0.05, 0.02, 0.01, 0.005), each given 30 seconds and 6 GB. Every returned tree's objective is recomputed independently from the tree and the raw data, and checked against the known optimum for that problem, so a solver cannot score by misreporting.

The hidden-set panel is the part that was not developed against. The ordering holds: the shipped model certifies 59 of the 70 held-out problems in 0.0132 ± 0.0007 s against GOSDT's 43 in 0.253 ± 0.014 s, so 16 more problems proved optimal and 19× faster, with the loop's own starting point, pygosdt, at 51 in 0.152 s and STreeD at 46 in 0.633 s. Three runs each, and the spread is small enough that nothing in that ordering is within noise.

Two things the held-out run shows that the development suite did not. GOSDT issues a second false certificate, on the stand-in for chudi at a penalty of 0.005: it reports 0.2518 as optimal where a tree scoring 0.2099 exists, in all three runs. That is independent of the tic-tac-toe case, so the unsound bound is a property of the implementation rather than one unlucky dataset. And STreeD's trees are marginally better on average here, 0.3366 against our 0.3371, while taking 48× as long and certifying 13 fewer problems: on the eleven problems neither solver certifies, STreeD spends its 30 seconds finding slightly better uncertified trees. Being fastest to a proof is not the same as being best without one.

At full size that last caveat does not survive, because the question stops being who finds a better uncertified tree and becomes who returns one at all. Given the same 14 datasets with every row and column kept and 30 minutes a problem, the shipped model certifies 9 of the 14 and returns a tree for all of them, at a geometric mean of 3.1 s; v49 does the same in 2.4 s. STreeD certifies 5 and returns nothing on 4, GOSDT certifies 4 and returns nothing on 9, and SPLIT, which is heuristic and certifies nothing anywhere, returns nothing on 10. The failures are memory, not time: GOSDT stops at the 6 GB cap on 8 of the 14, and STreeD is killed by the operating system on the widest dataset. The gap the mirror showed at 30 seconds is the same gap, and it widens once the problems are large enough to exhaust a machine.

Removing the time limit instead. The fourth panel gives the same 70 hidden problems 4 hours each. Every exact solver certifies more, and the ordering does not move: the shipped model goes from 59 to 64 of 70, the five new proofs taking between 4 and 61 minutes, and the 8-thread v46 also reaches 64 with v49 at 63 (v49 needs more than 4 hours on the iris stand-in at the smallest penalty, where v46 finishes in 92 minutes); pygosdt reaches 62, two of its eleven new certificates after 2.4 and 3.1 hours; GOSDT goes from 43 to 47, one of them after 3.3 hours; gosdt-guesses from 47 to 48; and STreeD stays at 46. Six problems are certified by nobody with 4 hours and 6 GB: the compas_processed and fico_1k stand-ins at the three smallest penalties, the two widest datasets with 625 and 1,357 binary splits. What ends the runs there is different for different solvers, and that is the finding. GOSDT reaches 6 GB on 20 problems, after 8 seconds to 31 minutes, and gosdt-guesses on 22, most within a minute; the shipped model and pygosdt reach it on 6 and 8, after 4 to 77 minutes of search; the 8-thread versions never do, staying at or under 1.1 GB on the six open problems for the whole 4 hours. STreeD goes both ways: 16 memory stops and 8 runs to the 4 hour mark.

3. Methods: AutoOptTree

The objective is the one GOSDT introduced,

minimise   misclassification rate + regularization * (number of leaves)

over every decision tree on the binarized features. Write R(X) for the optimum of that objective restricted to the rows that reach a node, its capture set X: the least, over all trees on X, of the cost-weighted loss on X plus λ per leaf. The solver is a memoised branch-and-bound over capture sets. A capture set is a bitmask over packed 64-bit words, so a subproblem reached by two paths is recognised and solved once.

What "exact" means, and what has to be proved. Every version keeps, for each subproblem it has touched, an interval [lb, ub] and a solved flag, and the final answer is a certificate rather than an incumbent because three invariants hold throughout:

  • (i) every stored lower bound is at most R(X);
  • (ii) every stored upper bound is the objective of a real tree on X, one the extraction rebuilds;
  • (iii) a call solve(X, budget) returns either with lb = ub = R(X), or with lb > budget, a proof that no tree on X fits the budget.

A version is exact if and only if every rule it adds preserves the three, which gives the audit below a short checklist. A change that only rearranges computation, a kernel, a cache, a compiled port, adds no rule and inherits exactness; its risk is a transcription error, not a theory error. A new lower bound needs a proof that it never exceeds R. A new way to stop early needs (iii). A shared or seeded incumbent needs (ii). And a rule that can discard a tree that might be optimal breaks exactness however good its measured results are. Read against that checklist, the loop's 41 recorded versions sort into the bounds, which is the only place theory enters; the parallel phase, which shares bounds and incumbents between threads; the seeds, which only touch upper bounds; the refactors; and the approximations. The bounds are stated and proved next, one collapsible each, and the ledger after them gives every version's verdict. Two verdicts disagree with the loop's own record, which is the reason to audit by proof rather than by test.

The bounds, and why each is admissible

Inherited from GOSDT: equivalent points, leaf support, look-ahead, threshold exchange

Equivalent points. Rows with identical binary features but different labels can never be separated by any split, so whatever the tree, each such class contributes at least the cost of its cheaper label. Summed over the classes captured by X this is a floor m(X) on the loss of every tree on X. A tree with any split has at least two leaves, so R(X) ≥ min(leaf(X), m(X) + 2λ), where leaf(X) is the cost of the best single label plus λ. This is the bound every child starts from.

Leaf support. Let pot(S) be the potential of a set of rows, the largest loss they could contribute (for 0/1 losses, their share of the rows). In an optimal tree every leaf has potential at least λ. Proof: take a leaf L with pot(L) < λ, delete the split above it and route its rows into the sibling subtree. The tree has one leaf fewer, saving λ, and its loss rises by at most pot(L), since a rerouted row can cost at most its potential; the change is negative. Two rules follow. A node with pot(X) < 2λ is a leaf, because any split gives a child of potential below λ. And, from v4, a split with a child of potential below λ is dropped: no optimal tree on X uses it, and none on any subset of X either, since potential only shrinks under subsets. A second leaf test, leaf(X) − m(X) < 2λ in the loss-only form loss(X) − m(X) < λ, is admissible for any nonnegative costs: a split costs at least m + 2λ and the leaf costs loss + λ. (The inherited test 1 − m(X) < λ assumes losses never exceed 1, which a user cost matrix can violate; v14 removed it.)

Look-ahead budgets. The two children of a split are solved in turn: the first with the budget less the second's lower bound, the second with the budget less the first's optimum. If the first returns with lb above its budget, the split costs at least lb(first) + lb(second), which is above the budget, and is discarded; the same for the second. Budget deepening (v9) changes only the sequence of budgets a child is given, geometric from its lower bound, and every call honours (iii), so the answer is unchanged.

Threshold exchange. R is monotone under inclusion: an optimal tree of X restricted to S ⊂ X has no more loss and, once leaves that capture no row of S are deleted, no more leaves, so R(S) ≤ R(X). For consecutive thresholds t < t′ of one numeric column, left(t) ⊇ left(t′) and right(t) ⊆ right(t′). So if lb(right(t)) is at least the leaf value of right(t′), the split at t′ is no worse than the split at t and t is dropped; symmetrically on the left. Dropping a split that another candidate dominates never removes an optimum, and the two rules exclude each other on a pair, so no cycle drops both.

Similar support across a whole column (v2)

For thresholds t < t′ of one column the two splits disagree on exactly the rows with t ≤ x < t′, and because the left sets are nested those rows have potential |pot(left(t)) − pot(left(t′))|. Any tree rooted at t′ becomes a tree rooted at t by moving those rows to the other side: the leaf count is unchanged and each moved row changes its loss by at most its potential. Hence |R(t) − R(t′)| is at most that potential, and a bound proved for one threshold lower-bounds every threshold of its column at a distance. The reference applies this to the two neighbouring thresholds; v2 applies it to the column in one vectorised update, by the same proof.

One column solved exactly by segmentation (v3)

A tree whose every split is a threshold on the same column partitions the rows into intervals of that column, one per leaf, and every partition into k intervals is realised by a chain of k − 1 threshold splits with the same loss and the same k. So the best such tree is the best segmentation of the rows, sorted by the column, into contiguous segments each charged its best single label plus λ: a quadratic dynamic programme over cumulative class counts the node already holds. When the thresholds of one column are the only splits that separate the node's rows, after the two exclusions above, both monotone under subsets so that a descendant cannot regain a split its ancestor lost, every optimal tree on X uses only that column and the programme's value is R(X) exactly; by optimal substructure the same holds for every node of the chain, which is stored solved. On any other node the chain is a real tree and its value is used only as an upper bound.

The pairwise stage: exact two-level trees, and the rule below 4λ (v7, v8, v10)

Counting rows for every pair of splits gives, for each child of each split, the class counts of the child's own two cells under every second split, hence the exact cost of the best two-leaf tree on that child, best2(child). Three things follow. (a) 2λ + best2 is achieved by a real tree, so min(leaf, 2λ + best2) is an upper bound on each child and the sum over a split's children an upper bound on the split. (b) Every tree on a child is a leaf, a single split, or has at least three leaves, so R(child) ≥ min(leaf, 2λ + best2, 3λ). (c) Every tree on X with at most three leaves is a leaf or a root split with a leaf on one side and at most two leaves on the other, a two-level tree, so the minimum over all splits of the two-level values, with the leaf, is exactly the optimum over trees with at most three leaves. When the budget is below 4λ no tree with four or more leaves fits, so that minimum is R(X) if it is within budget and otherwise a proof, with R(X) ≥ min(best, 4λ) the bound to store.

v7 stored something else in the failing case: the smaller of best and the cheapest rejected split's bound, which can lie at or below the budget, so a caller could take an unsolved node for a solved one. That violates (iii), and v7 is approximate. v8 stores min(best, 4λ), the quantity the proof gives. Note what (c) needs: the minimum must range over every split that could root an optimal tree, so the stage runs over every split that separates the node's rows, not only over the candidates that survive the budget filter. v49 returns to this.

The superset bound (v14)

For every child S of every split, R(X) ≥ R(S) ≥ lb(S), by the monotonicity used for threshold exchange: restricting an optimal tree of X to S loses no loss and no leaves beyond the empty ones. So a node's lower bound can be raised to the largest lower bound proved for any child of any split, at no cost. v14 also removed the inherited leaf test that assumes losses at most 1 and made a user-supplied upper bound a budget rather than an achievable value, closing two gaps a cost matrix could have exposed.

The shape relaxation (v15)

Let ga(S) be a lower bound on every tree with exactly a leaves on a set S: g1 = leaf(S) and g2 = 2λ + best2(S), both exact from the pairwise counts; g3 = 3λ plus the cheapest cell any split peels off S as a leaf, since a three-leaf tree has a leaf at depth one, which is such a cell, and the other side costs at least nothing; ga = aλ for a ≥ 4. Every tree on X with four or more leaves has a root split whose sides carry a and b leaves with a + b ≥ 4, so the minimum over all splits and all such (a, b) of ga(left) + gb(right) lower-bounds all of them at once (both sides at four or more leaves cost at least 8λ, where the minimum starts). If the best two-level tree is no worse than that minimum and no worse than the leaf it is optimal, since it already beats every tree with two or three leaves exactly, and the node is solved at any budget without expanding a child. The children's bounds are raised to min(g1, g2, g3, 4λ) by the same case split.

Triple counts and the depth-3 floors (v19, v20; off since v40), and the first finding

Counting rows for every triple of splits gives every child its exact best three-leaf tree and its exact best (2,2) tree, two-level on both sides, so a child's achievable value is the minimum over one, two, three and (2,2) leaves. The stage was gated to nodes with at most 14 candidates that fit in 64 words, with a budget below the equivalent-points floor plus 8λ, and v40 turned it off: under the compiled engine the expansions it saved cost less than its cubic count. Its lower bounds on the trees it does not enumerate are floors from the cells the triples expose. Four leaves other than (2,2) are a (1,3) or (3,1) shape, a cell as a leaf plus a three-leaf tree that has a leaf at its own depth one, a sub-cell: at least 4λ + leaf(cell) + cheapest sub-cell of the other side. Five leaves: (1,4) peels a cell; (2,3) a two-leaf tree on a cell plus a sub-cell. Six: (1,5) a cell, (2,4) a two-leaf cell, (3,3) a sub-cell on each side. All of these are admissible, and v19, which charged six or more leaves 6λ and nothing else, is exact.

The finding. v20 charges seven or more leaves "6λ plus the same floor", min(c3, c5, c6), and that is not admissible. A (3,4) tree can carry a (2,2) subtree on its four-leaf side and a (4,4) tree one on both, and in those shapes no leaf is a cell or a sub-cell, so nothing in c3, c5 or c6 bounds their loss, which can be zero. Concretely, take a node whose rows have three binary features and a label equal to their parity. Every cell (one feature fixed) and every sub-cell (two fixed) is half wrong, so with nX rows out of n all three constants equal nX/4n and the floor for seven or more leaves reads 6λ + nX/4n; but the eight-leaf tree that fixes all three features is perfect and costs 8λ, below the floor whenever nX > 8λn. On such a node v20 stores a lower bound above the optimum. That bound is written into the child's memo entry and, through the superset bound, into its parent's, from where it can prune a split that contains the optimum at a later budget: a false certificate. The loop's validation could not see it. Its exhaustive trials used at most three features, a child of a split has at most two left, and no two-feature subproblem holds a seven-leaf tree. On the 70 benchmark problems the gated stage never bound wrongly (every value it certified equals the known optimum), so the recorded numbers of v20 to v39 stand as measurements; their guarantee does not. The admissible floor for seven or more leaves is 7λ. The shipped package carries the stage disabled and now uses that floor, so enabling it is safe.

The pair stage over the candidates only (v49), and the second finding

On a wide node with few surviving candidates v49 counts pairs for the candidates against every other split instead of every pair. Most of its bookkeeping is right: a split without pair information keeps the bounds it had, its leaves are the only trees it is credited with, and in the shape relaxation every tree with four or more leaves rooted at it is charged that split's own bound, a valid lower bound on any tree rooted there. The rule below 4λ is where the restriction bites. Its proof (above) needs the minimum over the two-level trees of every split; with the restricted stage that minimum runs over the candidates, and a three-leaf tree rooted at a rejected split is not in it. A rejected split's value is at least its bound, which exceeds the budget, so the rule's success case still holds; but in the failing case v49 stores min(best, 4λ) where only min(best, 4λ, cheapest rejected bound) is justified, and the stored value exceeds R(X) whenever a rejected split has a three-leaf tree between the budget and that minimum. A memo entry above the optimum is the same hazard as before: it can prune the optimum at a later budget. v49 certified nothing wrong on either benchmark and its rows are reported as measured, marked; it is not the shipped version, and the omission is one term.

Parallel certification (v25, v29, v30, v32, v34)

After a short sequential phase the root's surviving candidate splits are handed to eight workers, processes in v25 and v29 and threads from v30 on. Exactness rests on what crosses between them. The incumbent is the value of a real tree and only decreases, so a split that a worker proves above the incumbent it saw is above the final incumbent, hence above the answer; every other split is solved exactly by the same search, and the root's optimum is the minimum over them and the leaf, as in the sequential loop. v32 shares lower bounds through a lock-free table keyed by the full capture mask and row count: a bound is only ever raised, a partly written key is a strict subset of the true one and so has a different row count, and every published value was proved by the same search on exactly that subproblem, so a reader sees a valid bound or nothing. v34 shares solved optima the same way and pushes an improved incumbent down a task's whole stack, re-deriving each child budget as when it was pushed; budgets only tighten, and a frame that ends above its budget returns unsolved with a sound bound, so the conclusions are the sequential ones.

Seeds, repair and budgeted stages (v43, v45, v46): exact whenever the search finishes

The lookahead-greedy seed (v43) walks down from the root, choosing each node's split by its expansion's best tree, and installs a subtree's value only when it beats what the node holds; every value it writes is the objective of the structure it writes, so it only ever supplies (ii). The bottom-up repair of v45 solves each internal node of the incumbent as a subproblem with its own value as the budget: by (iii) it ends solved or proven, and what it certifies is a sound memo entry. v46's budgeted pair stage, which under a short cap evaluates the k most promising splits only, derives no bound from its result (the frame is flagged, so the shape relaxation and the exact rule are skipped), and v43c's rule that skips a pair stage whose estimated cost exceeds a tenth of the time limit leaves bounds uncomputed rather than changed. None of this can produce a wrong certificate; what it produces under a cap is an incumbent, which is why one file is an exact solver at 30 s and an anytime method at 2 ms. A draft of v46 did certify two wrong trees: an indentation slip left the candidate refilter under the wrong branch, a transcription error of the kind the checklist predicts for refactors, caught by the run's own check and fixed before its rows were recorded. Drafts of v13 and v41 were caught the same way.

Guessed bounds and a depth budget (v47): approximate by construction

The gosdt-guesses recipe replaces the equivalent-points floor by a reference model's loss on the subproblem plus λ, and caps the depth. The reference loss is not a lower bound on anything, since a tree can beat the reference model on any subset, so subtrees that contain the optimum are pruned wherever the reference is poor (on monk_2 the regret reached 0.12 to 0.23), and a depth cap removes trees from the search space outright. Both break exactness by design; the row is reported for what it is.

"Latent gap" means the version ran as an exact solver and every value it certified on the benchmark equals the known optimum, but the argument for its guarantee has the hole described in the collapsible named. The "why" column is the collapsible that carries the proof; a refactor's "no new rule" is the whole argument, and its risk, a transcription error, is what the loop's exhaustive checks were for: three drafts (v13, v41, v46) were caught by them, and the two gaps above are the ones those checks could not reach.

Three lines of the audit are worth stating outside the collapsibles. The shipped model, v40 run on one thread, is exact with no reservation: every rule it applies is proved above. The versions from v20 to v39 and v49 ran as exact solvers and certified nothing wrong on any problem in this post, so their points in Fig 1 and their rows in Table 1 stand as measurements, but each carries a bound whose proof has a hole that the benchmarks never reached, and the table marks v49. And the approximations are three: v7, whose bug the loop found itself; v47, which guesses by design; and the anytime rows, which are the exact solver stopped early.

Two engineering facts complete the picture. Rows are ordered so that the capture sets of deep nodes occupy few words, which is free because the objective does not depend on the order of the rows. And the whole search is compiled: nodes are rows of arrays behind an open-addressing index, the recursion is an explicit stack of frames, and Python re-enters the compiled function in short chunks only to enforce the time and memory limits. The compiled search takes about 20 seconds to build the first time it runs on a machine; the result is cached on disk, so every later process loads it in about a second, and if the cache directory is unwritable OPTTREE_NUMBA_CACHE=0 skips caching and pays the build each time.

Rounds after the shipped version

The loop kept going after the version this package ships, and the three most recent rounds are worth reporting less for their speed than for what they found.

The published shortcut, measured inside our own search. gosdt-guesses gets its speed from two ideas: take the thresholds a boosted-stump model uses, and use that model's loss as a lower bound on each subproblem. The loop implemented both as options in this solver so they could be compared without the rest of the implementation getting in the way. Every configuration is dominated by the exact search itself: the full recipe reaches a mean regret of 0.011 at 0.0019 s. The reference bound is what costs: on monk_2 the stump ensemble is a poor reference, its guessed bounds prune subtrees that contained the optimum, and the regret reaches 0.12 to 0.23. The same held on three full-size datasets outside the suite. Guessing a bound is not free; it trades correctness for speed, and here it did so at a worse rate than a clock does.

The time was not in the search. Profiling full-size fits showed the search certifying COMPAS at a penalty of 0.02 in 0.2 seconds inside a fit that took 86 seconds. Nearly all of it was binarization: each of 64,859 rules applied as its own pandas operation, categorical rules as a Python loop over rows, and the whole thing done twice. One broadcast comparison per column brought the encoding from 82 seconds to 2.0, and finding the equivalent-point classes by hashing packed rows rather than sorting 3 KB records took another 2.6 seconds to 0.05. A full-COMPAS fit went from 86 seconds to about 5, with the suite unchanged because the search never moved. It is worth saying plainly: for a while the fastest exact tree search in this comparison spent 99% of its time preparing the data.

Restricting the pairwise stage. The pairwise stage looped over every splitting feature rather than only the surviving candidates, and each thread's workspace held 96 frames times the feature count, 634 MB on the full COMPAS table and the reason wide tables ran into the memory guard. Restricting the stage to the candidates and growing workspaces on demand made v49 the fastest exact version on both benchmarks and cut the workspace to 106 MB per thread. The first attempt ran the restricted stage everywhere and lost 1.5 to 1.8× on the widest problems; bounding it by the work it implies keeps the wins without the losses. The audit in the Methods section finds that one branch of the restricted stage stores a bound the restriction no longer justifies; it never fired, and v49 is not the shipped version.
